# Win the Fill, Not the Forecast

The minute-8 favorites bot will gain more from changing how it buys and how much it bets than from any new predictive signal. Its edge is real at the mid price: in the bot's own history, favorites priced at 90% or more won 1.2 to 1.9 points more often than priced. But crossing the spread and paying Kalshi's taker fee consumes most of that edge, and near 95c every 0.1c a contract moves the one-bet Kelly fraction (the bet size that maximizes long-run growth) by about 1.5 to 2 points. Kalshi charges no maker fee (the fee on resting orders) on any of the 16 series the bot trades, and it quotes them in 0.1c ticks above 90c. Studies of both Kalshi and Polymarket find that whoever posts the order, not whoever takes it, collects most of the favorite premium. A resting bid one tick above the best bid would save roughly 0.5 to 1.3c a contract, enough to double the held-out edge, as long as the bids that get filled do not lose much more often. The trade tape the repo is already pulling can test that before any live trial. Sizing is the second lever. The 13.5% Kelly figure is lifted by one strong month. The only period not used to choose the rules gives 9.1%, with a range that includes zero. On that basis the single 20% window cap runs at 0.6 to 0.8 of correlated Kelly in crypto-heavy windows, which implies roughly a 20-35% chance of eventually hitting the half-bankroll halt. A crypto sub-cap near 12% cuts that to about 12%. Third, the bot's own fills show that Kalshi charges this account fees to the hundredth of a cent, not rounded up to the cent as every backtest assumes (0.344c versus 0.56c a contract on live bets). So the measured edge is slightly understated, and smaller, fractional stakes carry no fee penalty. Fair-value models, cross-venue feeds and order-flow signals have the weakest support: the best tests on 15-minute crypto binaries found that they do not beat the market's own price. They are worth building mainly as a cancel trigger for resting bids. The edge is thin and probably being competed down, and the bot's 69 live bets over four trading days say nothing about it either way.

## Each tenth of a cent moves Kelly about two points

Almost all of the bias the bot harvests sits inside the bid-ask spread. The repo replicated it across every 15-minute market since Jul 18, measuring at the mid price. Favorites priced at 90% or more **won 95.7% of the time against 94.5% priced in crypto, 96.1% against 94.2% in metals, and 95.9% against 94.1% in energy**. Yet average spreads ran 2.0 to 2.7c, and with live-like fills no group cleared its costs over the whole history ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)). The outside evidence has the same shape. Across 72.1 million Kalshi trades, 95c contracts won 95.83% of the time ([Becker](https://www.jbecker.dev/research/prediction-market-microstructure)). Yet in Bürgi, Deng and Whelan's Kalshi sample, takers buying high-priced contracts "still lose money on average" after fees, while makers buying at 50c or more earned 2.6% ([BDW](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)). In zero-day S&P 500 options, more than 60% of retail losses were transaction costs rather than overpricing ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-04-21/day-traders-lose-358-000-per-day-gambling-on-zero-day-options)). A two-week live test of Kalshi's 15-minute books concluded that "the edge in a 15-minute market belongs to the spread, and the spread belongs to the market maker" ([OddsShopper](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/prediction-markets/kalshi-15-minute-markets)).

The arithmetic shows why cost dominates. For a binary bought at price c that wins with probability p, the Kelly fraction is (p − c)/(1 − c). Near 95c, **each 0.1c a contract moves the one-bet Kelly fraction by roughly 1.5 to 2 points** (my arithmetic). The repo shows this directly: adding 0.4c a contract of slippage to the unfiltered rule moves its Kelly from +6.3% to −1.5% ([key_stats.json](../research/kalshi_15min/key_stats.json)). The live rule's 13.5% Kelly corresponds to roughly 0.5 to 0.8c of net edge a contract, and the held-out 9.1% to about 0.5c. Against that, the taker fee alone costs about a third of a cent, and the spread costs more. The bot's careful work on filters, exits and halts moves Kelly by a few points; a single tick of execution moves it by two.

One cost is already lower than the backtests assume. Every backtest, and the bot's own `taker_fee()`, rounds the fee up to the cent per order ([bot.py](../bot/kalshi_bot/bot.py)). Kalshi's docs instead say that direct members' balances align to $0.0001 and that over-rounding is refunded across an order's fills ([Kalshi fee rounding](https://docs.kalshi.com/getting_started/fee_rounding.md)). The bot's live database (`bot/state/live-prod.db`) shows that this account is charged at sub-cent precision. Across 69 filled bets, Kalshi's reported `fee_cost` totals **$0.614, against $1.00 under cent rounding and $0.611 under the unrounded formula: 0.344c a contract versus 0.56c**. At the backtests' roughly $7 orders, cent rounding overstates fees by about 0.07 to 0.1c a contract, or about 1.5 Kelly points, so the 13.5% and 9.1% estimates are slightly conservative. More importantly, the repo's warning that small orders lose a noticeable share to rounding does not apply to this account. That removes the main obstacle to smaller and fractional stakes. Fractional contracts already trade in these books: the live fills add up to 178.5 contracts, and Kalshi's order endpoint accepts counts to two decimals ([Kalshi Create Order V2](https://docs.kalshi.com/api-reference/orders/create-order-v2.md)).

## Resting bids could double the edge, if fills are not poisoned

The most consistent finding in the literature is that the favorite premium goes to whoever posts the order. In Kalshi data from 2021 to April 2025, **makers bought 56.5% of all contracts traded at 90-99c**. Average returns were −9.64% for makers against −31.46% for takers. The authors' model explains why: traders with the most extreme beliefs choose to take, so takers do worse ([BDW](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)). Becker finds takers earning −1.12% and makers +1.12% across Kalshi, a gap that opened in 2024 as volume and professional liquidity arrived ([Becker](https://www.jbecker.dev/research/prediction-market-microstructure)). On Polymarket, favorites bought by posting an offer returned +0.641% equal-weighted, against −0.460% for favorites bought by accepting one. Dollar-weighted, the two are close (+0.816% versus +0.877%) ([Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12878v1)). Practitioners reached the same conclusion the hard way. In January 2026 Polymarket added taker fees to its 15-minute crypto markets to fund maker rebates, and in February it removed its taker speed bump ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/384461/polymarket-adds-taker-fees-to-15-minute-crypto-markets-to-fund-liquidity-rebates); [Protos](https://protos.com/polymarket-ends-trading-loophole-for-bitcoin-quants/)). One bot operator's lesson was "Always use limit orders" ([lkto1m](https://dev.to/lkto1m/february-2026-changed-polymarket-forever-heres-what-happened-to-my-bots-numbers-2fi5)). The main caveat cuts close to home: BDW find makers' advantage narrowing on closing-day prices, and a 15-minute market is all closing day.

Kalshi's own settings make the maker side unusually cheap here. All 16 series the bot trades report `fee_type: quadratic` with `fee_multiplier: 1` ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/series/KXBTC15M)), and Kalshi's docs reserve maker fees for series typed `quadratic_with_maker_fees` ([Kalshi Get Series](https://docs.kalshi.com/api-reference/market/get-series.md)). So **a resting fill on these series should pay no fee at all**. Even the repo's older assumption, a maker fee at 25% of the taker rate, would cost under 0.1c at 95c. There are no liquidity rewards to chase on the up/down series; the only 15-minute programs pay $20 a market on Coin Race ([Kalshi incentive programs](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/incentive_programs?status=active)). The competing quotes come from two designated crypto liquidity providers and one commodities provider, all under signed Market Maker Agreements ([Kalshi help](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/15410219-liquidity-provider-program)). The tick structure helps a small bidder. BTC and ETH 15-minute markets quote in **0.1c steps from 90c to $1**, while the queue at a single price can run to thousands of contracts: one BTC book showed 4,373 on the bid ([Kalshi API markets](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTC15M&status=open&limit=1)). Bidding one tick above the best bid puts the bot first in line at a cost of 0.1c, under a third of the fee it avoids. Changing an order's price afterwards sends it to the back of the queue ([Kalshi Amend Order V2](https://docs.kalshi.com/api-reference/orders/amend-order-v2.md)).

Compared with a taker fill at the ask, a filled bid one tick above the best bid saves the fee plus the spread less one tick. That is **roughly 0.5 to 1.3c a contract for spreads between 0.2c and 1c**, about 8 to 20 Kelly points and enough to double the held-out estimate (my arithmetic). The catch is who sells into that bid. Selling a favorite at 94.1c is the same as buying the longshot at 5.9c. In the seconds after minute 8, the trader most likely to do that is one who just saw the index move toward the strike. At 95c, **every point of lost win rate costs about 1c a contract**. So filled bids can win at most 0.5 to 1.3 points less often than the bot's taker bets before the maker route loses, which is about the size of the whole mid-price edge. No public data measures fill rates or adverse selection for resting orders in these markets. A vendor's sweep of maker variants on BTC 15-minute markets found only 26 of 100 positive, and they failed a permutation test (a check that results beat random chance), under assumptions the vendor flags as different from its taker tests ([TurbineFi](https://www.turbinefi.com/blog/5732-backtests-kalshi-bitcoin-15-minute-bots-2026)).

That makes the maker route an experiment, not a switch, and the test can start offline. The trade tape `liquidity.py` is already pulling records every print. For each historical rule bet, a hypothetical bid one tick above the minute-8 bid counts as filled if sell prints at or below it arrive before the bid expires. Comparing the would-fill subset's win rate with its price then measures adverse selection directly. The repo already lists maker orders as its fourth open step ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)). If the win-rate penalty is under about half a point, a live version would work like this. At the end of minute 8, for each favorite that passes the rule with a spread of at least 0.2c, it posts a `post_only` bid one tick above the best bid. Each bid carries an `expiration_time` inside the 45-second entry window and `cancel_order_on_pause`, and sits in an order group whose contract limit equals the intended size, so simultaneous fills cannot overshoot ([Create Order V2](https://docs.kalshi.com/api-reference/orders/create-order-v2.md); [Order Groups](https://docs.kalshi.com/getting_started/order_groups.md)). The bot cancels early if Kalshi's 5 Hz CF Benchmarks feed (or its Pyth feed for metals and energy) moves toward the strike, and falls back to the IOC when the bid expires ([Kalshi WebSocket channels](https://docs.kalshi.com/websockets/websocket-connection.md)). Bids should rest at least five seconds, because Kalshi already charges combo orders that rest under five seconds as takers and could extend that rule ([Kalshi changelog](https://docs.kalshi.com/changelog/index.md)).

Three of the bot's safety rules assume IOC-only orders and would need rewriting: one order per market from a ticker-derived ID, a halt whenever one of its orders is found resting, and no retries ([bot README](../bot/README.md)). A coin-flip A/B on each qualifying bet would then measure fill rate and price. The win-rate penalty has to come from the tape, because a one-point difference takes thousands of bets to see. Metals and energy stand to gain most: their spreads averaged 2.6 to 2.7c against crypto's 2.0c ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)).

The IOC path still needs fixing, because it leaves contracts on the table. The bot's first 76 real orders got 70% of the contracts requested and nothing on 17 (from the bot's repo), and the live database logs 25 skips as "nobody selling at that price". Two causes overlap. The research's $10 orders walked two to five price levels in ETH, NEAR and HYPE ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)), so an IOC priced at the best ask stops at the first level. And other traders take the ask before the order lands ([bot README](../bot/README.md)).

The bot polls Kalshi's REST API every three seconds (from the bot's repo). Subscribing to Kalshi's `orderbook_delta` WebSocket, and checking its sequence numbers, would let it fire at the minute boundary off a live book, removing up to three seconds ([Kalshi orderbook updates](https://docs.kalshi.com/websockets/orderbook-updates.md)). A server in AWS us-east-2, where monitoring places Kalshi's origin servers ([Glassnode](https://latency.glassnode.com/prediction-markets/about)), would save only about a tenth of a second more: a fresh REST call from the research machine took 146 to 169 ms to first byte. Raising the IOC limit to the best ask plus up to 0.4c, capped at 99.9c, lets the order walk the next few 0.1c levels while paying no more than the backtest already charges. It still fills at resting prices, and it stays within the bot's rule of halting only on a fill worse than the limit.

Before paying up for fills, `liquidity.py` should check whether the missed orders were better or worse bets than the filled ones. If asks vanish because fast traders grab the best favorites, more speed helps. If they vanish because market makers pull quotes ahead of a flip, the misses were a gift. The rest is housekeeping: the free Advanced rate tier, an explicit `exchange_index` of 2, and a kept-alive connection to `api.elections.kalshi.com`, which measured about 100 ms faster from here than the newer `external-api` host ([Kalshi rate limits](https://docs.kalshi.com/getting_started/rate_limits.md)).

## The held-out month says crypto exposure should shrink

The 13.5% one-bet Kelly comes from 20,009 historical bets, with a window-clustered range of 5.6 to 21.5%. But it averages three very different periods: 10.2% from Jul 18 to Aug 23 (range −3.5 to +24.0%), 18.7% from Aug 24 to Sep 23, and **9.1% from Sep 24 to Oct 8, the only stretch not used to choose the rules, with a range of −7.8 to +26.0%** (from the bot's repo). Two of the three periods sit near 9 to 10%. The Kelly literature is blunt about which error to fear. Mistakes in mean returns cost about ten times as much as mistakes in variances and twenty times as much as mistakes in covariances, and betting twice the true Kelly fraction drives growth to zero ([MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf)). If the true edge is half the estimate, full Kelly on the estimate grows nothing ([Thorp](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/cs286r/courses/fall12/papers/Thorpe_KellyCriterion2007.pdf)). Shrinking the stake for estimation error ([Baker & McHale](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/deca.2013.0271)) with the informal rule α = SR²/(SR² + 1) ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/TNWnK9g2EeRnQA8Dg/never-go-full-kelly)) gives about 0.92 of Kelly on the full-sample estimate, but about 0.53 on the held-out one (my arithmetic from the ranges above).

Correlation then sets the window total. When one crypto favorite loses, another in the same window loses 33% of the time instead of 4.9%. The same ratio is 7.8 in metals and 8.5 in energy, but only 1.1 between crypto and metals ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)). That implies a loss correlation near 0.3 within each family and almost none across them. Thorp shows that correlated simultaneous bets need sharply smaller per-bet fractions, and that the exact answer depends on the full joint distribution, not just the correlations ([Thorp](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/cs286r/courses/fall12/papers/Thorpe_KellyCriterion2007.pdf)). A serviceable approximation is f₁/(1 + (n − 1)ρ) per bet, where f₁ is the one-bet Kelly, n the number of bets and ρ their correlation. For this bot it lands within 4 to 8% of an exact log-growth solution (per the research notes' calculation). With f₁ = 9.1% and ρ = 0.3, **nine crypto favorites have a full-Kelly window total of about 24%**. When metals and energy are closed and only crypto qualifies, the current rule puts the whole 20% into that one correlated family, about 0.8 of Kelly. With four favorites at 3% each, it runs about 0.6. Thorp gives the chance that wealth ever halves when betting a fraction c of Kelly as 0.5^(2/c − 1). That puts the chance of eventually reaching the half-bankroll halt at **roughly 20 to 35% at those fractions, 12.5% at half Kelly and under 1% at quarter Kelly** ([Thorp](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/cs286r/courses/fall12/papers/Thorpe_KellyCriterion2007.pdf)), and that assumes the edge is known.

| Window | Current stake a bet (window total) | Half of correlated Kelly at 9.1% | Proposed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 crypto favorites | 3.0% (12%) | 2.4% (9.6%) | 2.5% (10%) |
| 9 crypto, metals and energy closed | 2.2% (20%) | 1.3% (12%) | 1.3% (12%) |
| 9 crypto + 5 metals + 2 energy | 1.25% (20%) | crypto 1.3% (12%), metals 2.1% (10%), energy 3.5% (7%) | crypto 1.3% (12%), metals and energy 1.1% (8%) |

The proposal replaces the single 20% cap with a 12% cap on crypto and an 8% cap on metals and energy together, with no bet above 2.5%. In full windows the total barely changes. The cut lands where the risk concentrates: crypto-only windows and small windows. The metals-and-energy cap sits below its theoretical half-Kelly level because those families' edges are the least certain; energy lost 6.5c a bet with live-like fills over the whole unfiltered history ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)). Two cross-checks agree. Risk-constrained Kelly is a method that caps the chance of a given drawdown ([Busseti, Ryu & Boyd](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.06183)). Requiring under a 5% chance of ever losing half allows about 14% for a 14-bet window, even on the 13.5% estimate (per the research notes' calculation). And the repo's replay of one crypto bet a window cut the worst window from −$110 to −$40 ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)). Smaller stakes would have been costly under cent rounding, where one contract pays a full cent of fee. Under the account's real $0.0001 precision with fractional counts, a $3 order pays the same fee per contract as a $10 one: 1.3% of a $250 bankroll is $3.25, or 3.4 contracts at 95c, which whole-contract rounding would cut to 3.

The halts should stay as circuit breakers rather than become profit levers. The repo found that losses don't come in runs: the four hours after a window with three or more losses averaged +6.0c a bet. It also found that a stop at half the starting money costs almost nothing when the edge is real ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)), which is what Kaminski and Lo predict when returns lack momentum ([Kaminski & Lo](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114876)). So the bot should keep the half-bankroll and 35%-in-24-hours halts and add no P&L pauses. The 8+-losses halt is best treated as a fault alarm for a person to review. The only window ever to lose more than seven bets was the Sep 16 Fed decision ([bot README](../bot/README.md)), now blacked out, and a full window can lose eight or more about 2% of the time with no change in edge (per the research notes' one-factor model, the Vasicek structure used for correlated loan defaults; see [vasicekfit](https://ftp.kaist.ac.kr/CRAN/web/packages/vasicekfit/refman/vasicekfit.html)). As the bankroll nears a floor, a Grossman-Zhou-style rule, which scales risk with the cushion above the floor, de-risks smoothly instead of stopping dead ([Grossman & Zhou](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v3y1993i3p241-276.html)).

Option-selling blowups add one more lesson. LJM Partners lost more than 80% in a week in February 2018 after, the SEC alleged, it raised risk during calm markets to chase return targets ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2021-89)). Dollar stakes should grow with the bankroll, but the fraction should never grow after a calm streak. The longer-term replacement is an exact per-window solve: maximize expected log wealth over a factor model of the window's losses, subject to the risk-constrained Kelly limit. That is a convex problem that runs in milliseconds for 16 bets ([Busseti, Ryu & Boyd](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.06183)).

## Forecasting signals rarely beat the market's own price

The best tests say external data does not beat the price. One open-source study used 1,720 KXBTC15M windows, choosing its gate on the first half and scoring it on an untouched second half. The Kalshi mid's Brier score (a calibration error measure, lower is better) was 0.1495, beating a volatility model's 0.1522. Buying the model's called side at the ask after fees returned −0.8c, +0.2c and −0.5c a contract at confidence gates of 0.85, 0.95 and 0.99. The author's verdict: "The calls are accurate, and the market already knows" ([darup67](https://github.com/darup67/kalshi-btc-agent)). On Polymarket's 15-minute BTC markets, a walk-forward model with 43 microstructure features, including Binance order flow, scored an AUC (how well it ranks outcomes; 1.0 is perfect) of 0.8377. The order book's own implied probability scored 0.8405, and simulated trading lost 0.116 payoff units a trade after costs. Polymarket quotes absorbed large Binance moves within a median 347 ms ([OpenMarket](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26245v1)).

Order-flow imbalance predicts crypto returns over seconds ([Bieganowski & Ślepaczuk](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00776)), but one- and five-minute imbalances have negligible power at longer horizons ([summary of arXiv 2607.09426](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2607.09426)). None of this forecasts a seven-minute outcome. Unmeasured claims of a 3-7 second Kalshi lag ([brandononchain](https://github.com/brandononchain/kalshibot)) conflict with the live test that found market makers repricing "within seconds" ([OddsShopper](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/prediction-markets/kalshi-15-minute-markets)).

Settlement mechanics still matter, both for any model and for risk. A crypto market settles Yes if the simple average of the 60 one-second CF Benchmarks index values in the minute before close is at least the same average from the minute before open. The strike is published as `floor_strike` at open, and ties go to Yes ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTC15M&status=settled&limit=1)). If index data is unavailable or incomplete at expiration, the market resolves No, a small risk that only Up favorites carry ([Kalshi CRYPTO terms](https://assets.kalshi.com/contract_terms/CRYPTO.pdf)). Averaging shortens the effective time to expiry by two-thirds of the averaging minute, to 6.33 minutes at entry. That lifts a 90% favorite priced on the naive seven minutes to 91.1% fair, a shift larger than the bot's whole edge (per the research notes' derivation; on averaged settlement see [Asian option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_option)). Fat tails cut the other way at the top. At equal variance, a Student-t with four degrees of freedom gives a favorite 1.28 standard deviations from the strike a 92.8% chance where a Gaussian says 90.0%, but gives one at 2.33 standard deviations 98.5% instead of 99.0% (per the research notes' calculation; see also [Andersen et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7488)). A naive Gaussian filter would therefore skip cheap 90-96c favorites and bless rich 99c ones.

The indexes themselves differ. Each is a depth-weighted mid of its constituent exchanges' order books, recomputed every 200 ms for BTC, ETH, SOL and XRP ([CF Benchmarks methodology](https://docs.cfbenchmarks.com/CME%20CF%20Real%20Time%20Indices%20Methodology.pdf)). BTC's index excludes Binance. **BNB, ZEC and NEAR use only Coinbase and Kraken, and HYPE adds Bitstamp; none includes those coins' main venues** ([CME CF constituents](https://docs.cfbenchmarks.com/CME%20CF%20Constituent%20Exchanges.pdf); [CF constituents](https://docs.cfbenchmarks.com/CF%20Constituent%20Exchanges.pdf)). Metals and energy settle on a single Pyth one-minute candle close with no averaging ([Kalshi API, gold](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXGOLD15M&status=settled&limit=1)), so the full seven minutes of variance applies, plus a single print's noise. Single-print settlement is what made Polymarket's five-minute markets manipulable: 821 wallets took $8.2M by pushing spot across the strike in the final seconds, though there was little evidence of this in 15-minute markets ([crypto.news](https://crypto.news/polymarket-five-second-exploit-manipulation-twap-fix/)). WTI is odder still. A vendor reports that Kalshi's named Pyth oil feed has been frozen since March and that settlements track the front-month future, which rolls early; this is unverified ([PredictionMarketsPicks](https://predictionmarketspicks.com/articles/how-kalshi-15-minute-markets-settle)).

What's worth building is a distance-to-strike measure. OddsShopper checked 200 BTC windows: 31 flipped late, 23 of them with the losing side at 70c or more two minutes out. "Wide" windows, those finishing at least $50 from the line, flipped 5 times in 754 ([OddsShopper](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/prediction-markets/are-kalshi-15-minute-bitcoin-markets-rigged)). That argues for measuring a favorite's safety as its distance from the strike in volatility units over the effective time to expiry. The bot's late-favorite and jump filters approximate this from prices. The repo's CSVs hold no spot prices or strikes. But settled Kalshi markets expose `floor_strike` and `expiration_value` for calibration, Kalshi's WebSocket streams the settlement indexes as `cfbenchmarks_value_5hz` and `pyth_value` ([Kalshi WebSocket](https://docs.kalshi.com/websockets/websocket-connection.md)), and a mean of Coinbase and Bitstamp prices tracked BTC's settlement value with a root-mean-square error of $8.77 ([darup67](https://github.com/darup67/kalshi-btc-agent)). The test that matters is whether, inside the 90-99.9c band, model probability minus the ask predicts net return out of sample, with window-clustered errors. Given the two nulls above, expect a small filter at best. The sure payoff is as the cancel trigger for resting bids.

Several cheap conditional tests remain, and none should change the rule on literature alone. On macro releases, a Federal Reserve Board paper finds that crypto reacts "sharply and near-instantaneously" to FOMC, CPI and payroll releases, with effects lasting up to 30 minutes ([summary of Kroner, Mohammed & Vega](https://rwatimes.substack.com/p/how-do-cryptocurrencies-price-economic)). An older New York Fed paper found bitcoin orthogonal to macro news ([Benigno & Rosa](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1052.pdf)). The repo found nothing consistent around the 8:30 and 10:00 ET releases, but its worst window ever was the Sep 16 Fed decision ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)). Extending the blackouts to CPI and payroll mornings is cheap insurance, not an edge.

On entry timing, late in sports events favorites become underpriced. On more than 200,000 Betfair matches, takers earned "small but statistically significant profits" backing strong favorites in the second half ([Whelan](https://www.karlwhelan.com/Papers/Betfair.pdf)). Kalshi sports prices turned step-like in the final ten minutes, with Platt slopes (above 1 means favorites underpriced) of 1.62 to 4.56 ([Moshrefi](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430)). But late BTC calls at minute 13 lost 2.2c a contract ([darup67](https://github.com/darup67/kalshi-btc-agent)), and practitioners say the final seconds belong to latency bots and blown-out spreads ([lkto1m](https://dev.to/lkto1m/my-polymarket-trading-bot-strategy-across-11717-trades-5m15m1h-framework-explained-3gbl)). The repo's `minutes.py` already scores every entry minute with an out-of-sample check; rerunning it with the live filters and true fees beats importing sports evidence.

On side, Becker's "optimism tax" (NO beats YES at 69 of 99 price levels) predicts that Down favorites, whose longshot is a YES, are more underpriced than Up favorites ([Becker](https://www.jbecker.dev/research/prediction-market-microstructure)). No one has tested this on 15-minute markets. The bot's live losses, 8 of 9 on Down favorites with 7 of them in two windows, show only that one up-move flips every Down favorite at once. In the top band, precise fees make 98-99.9c viable on fees alone (0.035c a contract at 99.5c). But Polymarket's probability error shrinks to as little as +0.11 points at the top of the range ([Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12878v1)), and one loss there erases 100 or more wins, so `by_band_fees.py` should be rerun with true fees first. Finally, HYPE lost 17.2c a bet in the repo's full sample, which the repo judged chance-level. Its thin index makes it worth watching, not cutting.

## A thin, crowded edge needs market-wide monitoring and more history

The direction of travel is against takers of favorites. Kalshi's maker-taker gap reversed sign in 2024 as quarterly volume jumped from $30M to $820M, and the 91-99c share of taker volume fell from 40-50% in 2021-23 to under 20% in 2025 ([Becker](https://www.jbecker.dev/research/prediction-market-microstructure)). BDW's bias coefficient fell from 0.048 in 2024 to 0.021 in early 2025 ([BDW](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)). On Polymarket, the wallets that buy favorites most persistently earn +0.25%, against +0.88% for everyone else, and capture just 4.8% of the net favorite gain ([Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12878v1)). The strategy is crowded. A reverse-engineered Polymarket account sweeps 94-99c asks in the final two minutes, in clips of about $2, across roughly 100,000 markets ([gabigol analysis](https://dev.to/fatherson/reverse-engineering-gabigol-high-frequency-crypto-updown-farmer-on-polymarket-d0a)). Public Kalshi bots run "Resolution Rider" and "Certainty Scalp" variants ([reedjacobp](https://github.com/reedjacobp/kalshi-trading-bot); [hamad-khawaja](https://github.com/hamad-khawaja/kalshi-trading-bot)). A vendor backtest of 4,904 BTC 15-minute strategies with realistic costs found only 2.1% profitable ([TurbineFi](https://www.turbinefi.com/blog/do-automated-trading-bots-make-money-prediction-markets-2026)). A self-described market maker in these contracts argues that favorite buyers sell volatility 5-10 points below mid ([Bits, Bytes and Bets](https://bitsbytesandbets.substack.com/p/problems-with-probability)). The same blog labels an earlier post as parody, and the repo's mid-price calibration contradicts the claim, but the warning about taking prices is sound.

The counterforce is new retail flow. Coinbase began offering Kalshi-operated 15-minute and one-hour markets on ten assets around Oct 6 ([DigitalToday](https://www.digitaltoday.co.kr/en/view/111471/coinbase-launches-ultra-short-term-betting-market-will-bitcoin-rise-or-fall-in-15-minutes)). Kalshi's BTC 15-minute market traded 6.74 billion contracts in September, and 15-minute markets produced $20.4M of Kalshi's $25.1M non-sports fees in the week to Oct 5 ([Bitcoin.com](https://news.bitcoin.com/igaming/kalshis-15-minute-gold-bets-top-ether-fast-markets-drive-fees/)). Becker's makers profit "by accommodating biased flow" ([Becker](https://www.jbecker.dev/research/prediction-market-microstructure)). More phone-based takers mean more bias to harvest, and most of it will go to whoever is resting on the book.

The live record cannot referee any of this. The live database holds 69 settled bets worth −$12.26, recorded on only four days (Sep 28-29 and Oct 9-10). Nine bets lost where the backtest's win rate implies about three, but 7 of the 9 came in two windows, which is what correlated losses look like. The bot README estimates two to three and a half months to tell a +4c edge from luck ([bot README](../bot/README.md)), and telling a 1.0% edge from a 1.1% one takes about two million coin tosses ([MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf)). The faster referee is the market itself. The bot can replay the live rule on every 15-minute market each day (about 240 qualifying favorites a day in the 20,009-bet history) and keep a posterior on each family's win-rate excess over price, with a forgetting factor so old data counts less. Stakes then scale with that posterior and shrink toward zero as the excess approaches the fee break-even. Even this signal is noisy: a week of market-wide bets pins the loss rate down only to about half a point before clustering, so the posterior should weigh about a month. Unlike P&L stops, it responds to evidence that the edge itself is fading.

The cheapest way to strengthen every estimate in this report is more history, and here the sources conflict. In the bot's own historical data, the 15-minute series it trades begin on Jul 18, 2026, which the repo treats as the first market ([repo README](../research/kalshi_15min/README.md)). Outside sources point earlier for crypto. A contract page shows `KXBTC15M-26APR210930-30`, an April 21, 2026 window ([simplefunctions](https://simplefunctions.dev/markets/KXBTC15M-26APR210930-30)). Robinhood lists a BTC 15-minute event dated Mar 9, 2026 ([Robinhood](https://www.robinhood.com/us/en/prediction-markets/crypto/events/btc-up-or-down-15-minutes-mar-09-2026-d07e84ce/)). Bitcoin.com cites ETH 15-minute volume estimates for January 2026 ([Bitcoin.com](https://news.bitcoin.com/igaming/kalshis-15-minute-gold-bets-top-ether-fast-markets-drive-fees/)). And crypto rose from under 5% of Kalshi volume earlier in 2026 to 21% on Aug 4 ([The Event Horizon](https://nexteventhorizon.substack.com/p/the-15-minute-ification-of-prediction-markets)). This report does not resolve the conflict. The Jul 18 start could be an artifact of how the history was fetched, or the earlier listings could belong to a different series. A direct API lookup of the April ticker, plus a settled-market query with closes before Jul 18, would settle it in minutes. If the markets exist, months of earlier crypto data would give the rule a second untouched test, which is worth more than any filter.

## Conclusion

The favorite-longshot bias is a payment from people buying lottery tickets to people willing to sell them. A resting bid on a 95c favorite is the same order as a resting offer of the 5c longshot. That puts the bot exactly where the lottery demand arrives: retail takers leaning toward YES and toward cheap contracts. Today, instead, it competes with designated market makers and latency bots for the few stale asks left at minute 8. The bot's filters, exits and halts are already well researched. What remains is to stop paying the toll it was built to collect.

The order of operations follows from the evidence. The fee-model fix and the sizing cut are code changes for today. The tape-based maker test decides this week whether the bot's next phase is as a liquidity provider, and the market-wide monitor decides how long any of it lasts. If filled bids win nearly as often as taker bets, the per-contract edge could rise from about half a cent to well over a cent. If they suffer heavy adverse selection, that is the most useful finding of all, because it would mean the minute-8 taker edge is as good as this strategy gets, and sizing discipline becomes the whole game.
