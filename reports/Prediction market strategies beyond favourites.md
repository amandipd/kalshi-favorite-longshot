# Settlement averaging leads a thin new field

Beyond the favourite-longshot bias, few trading strategies have shown a profit after fees for a taker. That bias means cheap unlikely bets are overpriced and heavy favourites are underpriced. A taker is a trader who fills orders already on the book, as this bot does. The strongest new lead for this bot is a settlement rule, not a pricing bias. Kalshi's hourly bitcoin and ether contracts pay out on the average of 60 one-second index prices before the close. Trading stays open during that minute ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTCD)). Once part of the average is locked in, the result is far more certain than a spot-price model assumes. Such a model treats the live price at the close as the final settle value. One open-source test found that a live estimate of the settle beat Kalshi's own prices in that last minute. It covered only 12 settlements, so it is a lead, not proof ([GitHub PR #160](https://github.com/JihuiTanUchicago/trading-rsi/pull/160)). The second lead is a category bias. In "single-name" markets, about one person or company, Yes costs **about 4.5 cents more than its real chance of winning**, before fees ([Bartlett and O'Hara](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f243677.pdf)). The rest of the field is weak. Options and futures have not out-predicted Kalshi. Price moves look close to random. Linked contracts almost never break the rules by more than the fees. AI forecasters beat Kalshi prices by a sliver, and most real-money AI tests lost. Every idea here is a test for after the fixed 6-week run ends on 27 November 2026. A check on 26 October confirmed that the bot's own 15-minute markets settle the same way, with trading open through the averaging minute.

## Options and futures have not out-predicted Kalshi yet

The idea is to price Kalshi off a bigger market. A Kalshi "above X" contract pays $1 if a price ends above a strike, the level named in the contract. An option on the same asset prices the same bet. So option prices give implied odds: the chance of each outcome, read off those prices. Several open-source projects now trade Kalshi bitcoin and S&P 500 contracts against these odds. **None has a finished result after fees on fresh data**, as of 26 October 2026.

The best early number cuts against the idea. The "Implied Odds" project compares Kalshi with Deribit bitcoin options and Cboe index options. It counts fees and trades only gaps of 3 cents or more. Where the two disagreed by that much, Kalshi was slightly more accurate than the options (Brier score 0.126 against 0.130). A Brier score measures how far forecasts miss, so lower is better ([Implied Odds](https://github.com/tavelarocque/implied-odds-research)). Its bitcoin paper trader uses no real money. It swung from +$2,074 to −$90 and back to about +$680 in one week. About half the quoted Kalshi size was gone 18 seconds after each snapshot. The project's pre-registered verdict is due **30 November 2026**. Pre-registered means its rules were fixed before the deciding data existed. It is the first fair test due, and it is worth reading before building anything.

Waiting for outcomes alone will not settle this. A second project ran a power study, which asks how often a test would catch a real effect. Option odds include risk pay: extra return that traders demand for carrying risk. After 2,500 trading days, a test using results alone caught that gap only 7% to 26% of the time ([japark22](https://github.com/japark22/kalshi-mispricing-engine)). In plain words, outcome-only backtests (tests on past data) would take decades here. Only side-by-side price comparisons, taken at the same moment, can find an edge in useful time. An edge is profit per contract, in cents.

Real-money tries have been small and flat. One bot priced Kalshi bitcoin contracts from Coinbase prices and recent swings. It traded real money in March 2026 and ended with a small net loss, by its author's account ([Nadimul96](https://github.com/Nadimul96/kalshi-vol-edge)). Its lessons fit this bot. Kalshi settles bitcoin on BRTI, the CF Benchmarks index built from several exchanges. A position priced right against Binance alone lost $32 against BRTI. Coinbase alone sat **$17.31 from BRTI on average, and up to $52.38 away**. It landed on the wrong side of the strike in 2 of 30 settled markets. Another project compared the swings implied by Kalshi prices with Deribit's 30-day volatility index, a gauge of expected price swings. The two differed far beyond luck ([milanroundraise](https://github.com/milanroundraise/kalshi-mispricing)). Most of that gap came from using a 30-day gauge on contracts that last hours. Its trading rule won 24% of bets, and five trades made 58% of the profit. Fees were left out. A 2026 journal paper adds that Kalshi bitcoin contracts carry their own risk pay, only loosely tied to Deribit's ([Lee, Lee & Lee](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521926003601)). So the two venues need not agree, even when both are fair.

The one steady finding is in the far tails, the least likely outcomes. Kalshi S&P range midpoints, halfway between the buy and sell prices, add up to $1.20 to $1.40 instead of $1. Far-out ranges barely trade, so their midpoints overstate tiny chances ([ethan2boz007](https://github.com/ethan2boz007-a11y/sp500-options-vs-kalshi)). That is the favourite-longshot bias again: small chances priced too high. Options add little new there.

Fed and inflation markets tell the same story. A Federal Reserve staff paper studied Kalshi data since 2022. The day before each Fed meeting, Kalshi's middle forecast had a perfect record. It missed the rate by 0.000 percentage points on average ([NBER w34702](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34702/w34702.pdf)). Fed funds futures, where big investors bet on the Fed's rate, missed by 0.010 percentage points. On headline inflation, Kalshi missed by 0.063 percentage points against 0.081 for the Bloomberg forecaster average. A separate study of 37 Fed meetings found Kalshi more accurate than futures at 25 of them (Brier score 0.0117 against 0.0126; lower is better) ([Lucidate](https://lucidate.substack.com/p/a-dollar-bet-has-called-the-fed-as)). Kalshi also beat the Cleveland Fed's inflation nowcast, a live model estimate of the coming number, over 56 releases (Brier score 0.065 against 0.101). So "trade Kalshi toward the futures price" means betting against the better forecaster.

A data vendor did find steady gaps between Kalshi and futures in 2026. They were 1.7 to 2.8 basis points, or hundredths of a percentage point ([BMLL](https://www.bmlltech.com/news/market-insight/when-kalshi-and-fed-funds-futures-disagree)). Its toy strategy made money in two meetings, but it hedged with futures. Kalshi had **about 1/140th of the futures market's depth**, the money you can trade before moving the price. With a few thousand dollars, the trade shrinks to a one-sided bet that futures are right. Across BMLL's two meetings, that bet won once and lost once.

Commodity markets have no outside study at all. Kalshi's oil contract settles on the official WTI futures settlement price ([Kalshi API, KXWTI](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXWTI)). Its daily gold contract settles on a one-minute Pyth price candle at 5:00 PM Eastern ([Kalshi API, KXGOLDD](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXGOLDD)). Options could price both in principle, but nobody has published a test. The project's own AAA gas result, which passed on fresh data twice, is still the best guide here. **For this bot, pricing off other markets is a wait-and-see idea.**

## A 60-second settlement average nearly decides the last minute

Kalshi's own rules spell out the mechanism. Hourly and daily bitcoin contracts pay on the simple average of BRTI over the 60 seconds before the hour ([Kalshi API, KXBTCD](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTCD); [Kalshi BTC terms](https://assets.kalshi.com/contract_terms/BTC.pdf)). BRTI is the CF Benchmarks bitcoin index, rebuilt every second from several exchanges. The settle is the final number a contract pays on. The same market data show trading closes at the settle time itself, for bitcoin and ether alike. So **trading stays open through the whole averaging minute**. A vendor note points out the trap for anyone watching spot, the live exchange price. A price above the strike, the level named in the contract, at :59 can still settle No if most of the minute sat below ([predictionmarketspicks](https://predictionmarketspicks.com/articles/how-kalshi-settles-bitcoin)). That vendor sells subscriptions.

An average moves less than a price. With 30 seconds left, half the minute is already fixed. Only the last 30 one-second prices can still move it, and each counts for one-sixtieth. The table shows my own arithmetic. It assumes bitcoin near $90,000 and yearly swings of about 38%. That is DVOL, Deribit's bitcoin volatility gauge, on 20 October 2026 ([cryptoticker](https://cryptoticker.io/en/bitcoin-price-prediction-volatility-premium-20-october-2026/)). "Typical miss" means how far the final settle usually lands from the best current guess (one standard deviation). A spot-price model is one that treats the price at the close as the settle.

| Time left before the close | Typical miss, counting the averaging | Typical miss, spot-price model | How much the spot model overstates the risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 minutes (about when the bot buys today) | about $117 | about $123 | about 5% too wide |
| 30 seconds | about $10 | about $33 | about 3 times too wide |
| 10 seconds | about $2 | about $19 | about 10 times too wide |

Kalshi's rules confirm the averaging for both the hourly and the 15-minute contracts, so every row applies to the bot's own markets. In the last half-minute, a strike $20 to $30 from the running average is close to decided. A trader who prices off spot alone sees far more risk than exists. That gap is the trade.

One open-source test tried it ([GitHub PR #160](https://github.com/JihuiTanUchicago/trading-rsi/pull/160)). It rebuilt BRTI from seven exchanges, with a typical error of about $4. It then estimated the settle live in the last 60 seconds of 12 hourly bitcoin settlements, on 24 and 25 October 2026. **Its estimate was far more accurate than Kalshi's midpoint price** (Brier score 0.03 against 0.13; a Brier score measures forecast misses, and lower is better). The midpoint is halfway between the best buy and sell quotes. A taker is a trader who fills orders already on the book. The test's taker simulation filled at the ask, the cheapest offer to sell, one second late. Its 13 trades netted about 58 cents in total after taker fees, one contract each time. The author warns that 12 settlements prove nothing yet, and plans a rerun at about 100.

Three things suit this bot. Its server sits in the same Amazon data-centre region (AWS us-east-2) as Kalshi's. It already trades bitcoin markets settled on BRTI. And in the last minute, prices sit near 97 to 99 cents, where the taker fee is about 0.2 cents or less. The taker fee is Kalshi's charge for trading, biggest near 50c. So a gap of 1 to 2 cents could survive fees, if the size is there to fill.

Three things cut against it. First, it is a speed race. In the Implied Odds data, half the quoted size vanished within 18 seconds, so others chase the same quotes ([Implied Odds](https://github.com/tavelarocque/implied-odds-research)). Second, the index rebuild must stay within a few dollars of the official one. Coinbase alone averaged $17 off. Third, the bot's own data warn about late entry. The project's 11 October backtest was a test on past data. In it, plain favourite buying (the likely side) from minute 10 onward lost money in both halves of the data. A last-minute rule must earn its money from model gaps, not from high prices alone.

**The first check is done.** On 26 October 2026, I read the rules of a settled 15-minute bitcoin market from Kalshi's API ([Kalshi API, KXBTC15M](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTC15M)). It settles on the simple average of 60 one-second BRTI prices before the close. The strike is the same kind of average, taken over the minute before the open. The market's close time is the end of that minute, so trading stays open through it. This also confirms the set-up of the project's 11 October fair-value backtest. So the idea applies to the bot's own markets. Those settle every 15 minutes, so a test sample grows four times faster than with hourly contracts. Today's entry is barely affected. Seven minutes out, averaging trims the settle's swing by only about 5%.

Other settlement rules offer less. Kalshi's hourly and daily S&P 500 contracts stop trading at 4:00 PM Eastern, the moment of the official close ([Kalshi API, KXINXU](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXINXU)). So there is no last-minute window to trade. Kalshi also names itself the "Source Agency" for the index value, a small rules risk ([Kalshi INX terms](https://assets.kalshi.com/contract_terms/INX.pdf)). Daily gold settles on one Pyth one-minute price at 5:00 PM Eastern. Whether that minute is thin and drifts from the futures price is untested.

Carry is the one nearby idea not worth a test. Carry means earning a return just for holding a position to its end. Kalshi pays **3.50% a year** on cash and positions, at a variable rate. Accounts need a $250 average monthly balance ([Kalshi help](https://help.kalshi.com/navigating-the-exchange/your-portfolio/apy-on-kalshi)). On Polymarket, near-certain long-dated contracts trade at a discount worth about 3% to 7% a year ([Gebele and Matthes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31431)). That looks like pay for tying up money, much like a Treasury bill, a short US government loan. The study reports no realised returns and no failure rate. A bot that recycles its bankroll every 15 minutes should not lock cash up for months. The useful point is smaller: the bot's idle cash already earns interest if the account qualifies.

## Price paths look random, and the real patterns die in the spread

This section asks whether price moves predict later price moves. Momentum means prices keep going the way they moved. Mean reversion means they snap back. Across old and new markets, the answer is mostly neither.

The classic study is Tetlock's 2004 look at TradeSports, an older betting exchange. In non-sports contracts, a past move predicted nothing at all (t = 0.03). A t-stat measures how far a result is from luck, and about 2 is the usual bar ([Tetlock 2004](http://www.columbia.edu/~pt2238/papers/Tetlock-Efficient_Info_Markets-01_02.pdf)). In sports contracts, prices gave back 13.8% of each move, just past the luck bar (t = −2.04). His best fade rule bet against big 30-minute moves near the end of games. It made +0.49 cents per contract after the old commission. Its gross profit could still have been luck: the likely range ran from −1.69 to +4.27 cents (95% CI, the range the true number is probably in). Kalshi's taker fee alone is 1.75 cents at a 50c price. The taker fee is Kalshi's charge for trading, biggest near 50c. That rule would lose on Kalshi today.

Newer data agree. A hobby study of 112,636 Polymarket markets ran 66 rules fixed in advance ([polylab](https://github.com/benaticak-alt/polylab)). It used a train/test split: build the rule on older data, score it once on newer data. Seven-day momentum and reversal rules came out flat or negative after a 1-cent cost. A research vendor found a Polymarket mean-reversion rule that looked strong at zero cost. A 0.1% cost per trade flipped its best version from a strong gain to a strong loss (Sharpe ratio, return per unit of risk, from +2.97 to −2.60) ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/exploiting-mean-reversion-in-decentralized-prediction-markets-evidence-from-polymarket-binary-contracts/)). It used three hand-picked markets, and Quantpedia sells research subscriptions.

Big trades do partly bounce back. On Polymarket, about 55% of a typical trade's price move reverses later ([Polymarket-v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04217v1)). But the part that reverses is tiny, **about 0.18 cents per contract**. That is smaller than Kalshi's taker fee at most prices. Only a maker, a trader who posts a resting order and waits, could collect it. The project's own resting-order test shows how small that prize is. At best, a resting order beat the bot's taker fills by only +0.32 cents for every dollar bet. Taker fills are buys from orders already on the book.

Live sports show underreaction, not overreaction. A 2026 study of 1,438 Kalshi NBA games matched prices to a play-by-play win model ([Angelini and De Angelis](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07811)). When the model's win chance moved, the price moved only about 64 cents per dollar of change in the same minute. It caught up over the next 5 to 15 minutes. In the last five minutes of close games, the same-minute response fell to about 51 cents. A follow-the-model rule earned **+0.87 cents per contract at the midpoint**, halfway between the buy and sell price. Paying the spread turned that into **a loss of 0.36 to 1.20 cents**. The spread is the gap between the buy and sell price, what it costs to get in. That loss was before Kalshi's fee. A second study of 41.8 million Kalshi NBA and NFL trades also finds slow, incomplete moves after scores ([Lee, Lee and Lee](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6964226)).

This clashes with older work. Tetlock found sports underdogs that had just dropped below 50c bounced back, worth +10.2% a game after costs in 2003 data. The project's own test of buying the underdog of a weak price band lost money. The newer Kalshi data side with the project.

"Will X happen by date" markets have a known long-range tilt. On Intrade, prices more than 100 days out were pulled toward 50c, so cheap Yes was too dear ([Page and Clemen](https://people.duke.edu/~clemen/bio/Published%20Papers/45.PredictionMarkets-Page&Clemen-EJ-2013.pdf)). Their trading rules earned 5.25% to 9.55% before fees. A 15% yearly charge for tied-up money wiped out one rule, and 25% wiped out the other. Kalshi now pays about 3.5% a year on positions, which shrinks that reason for the tilt. The popular "buy No on everything" idea rests on a base rate: 73.3% of resolved Polymarket markets end No ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/364381/polymarket-bot-bets-no-has-a-point)). A base rate is not a return, because the price already reflects it.

Thin hours bring more noise. In UK horse racing, weekend prices were a weaker guide to results than weekday prices ([Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/339957)). In Polymarket's 5-minute bitcoin markets, cycles flagged as pushed were 56% overnight against 40% normally, and 44% on weekends against 27% ([Dai, Jia and Yu](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.31675v1)). No study measures hour-of-day effects on Kalshi. The project's own backtest is the best data here. Skipping the 9:30 ET window held up out of sample, meaning on data the rule never saw while being built. Other hours and weekends were noise.

The last hours are where prices swing most. Across 41,348 Kalshi contracts, swings rose sharply near the end and peaked near 50c ([Xi, Moallemi, Pai and Wang](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08199)). They follow a simple rule: price × (1 − price) ÷ time left. That gives the bot a yardstick for a normal late move, and moves far bigger than it are worth flagging. This is risk control, not a new edge (profit per contract, in cents).

The sharpest late reversals come from settlement pushes. In Polymarket's 5-minute bitcoin markets, traders pushed the reference spot price, the exchange price the contract settles on, in the final seconds ([Dai, Jia and Yu](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.31675v1)). Where one side was priced 90% to 100%, **pushed cycles flipped 34% of the time, against 1% otherwise**. Some 821 wallets took $8.2 million. The effect was much weaker in 15-minute contracts. A 60-second average also blunts any one-second push. Both facts favour staying on 15-minute markets, not moving to 5-minute ones. On Kalshi's own 15-minute bitcoin markets, late flips depend on distance. Windows ending $50 or more from the start price flipped in the last two minutes about 1% of the time. Windows ending within $50 flipped about 21% of the time ([OddsShopper](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/prediction-markets/are-kalshi-15-minute-bitcoin-markets-rigged)). That site uses affiliate links. **For this bot, no price-path pattern is worth a taker test.**

## Linked contracts agree, but single-name Yes runs 4.5 cents rich

Related contracts must obey simple rules. In a ladder of "above X" contracts, a higher strike (the price level named in the contract) can never be worth more than a lower one. In a bracket, a set of ranges where exactly one wins, prices should add to about $1. When they break these rules, arbitrage appears: a set of trades that locks in a profit whatever happens. On Kalshi, these breaks almost never survive the costs.

The one careful Kalshi screen polled 382 contracts in 28 events each minute for 85 minutes ([nnotyan](https://github.com/nnotyan/kalshi-bracket-pricing)). A naive check flagged a sure-profit set in 32.7% of event-minutes. On well-quoted events, 97.1% of those flags were fake. They came from stale quotes, or from sets that did not cover every outcome. **After depth, staleness and fees, zero survived.** Depth is how much money you can trade before moving the price. The biggest real gap was a 14-contract GDP bracket buyable at 98c for a $1 payout. After fees it lost 4.25 cents at 1,000 contracts, and 12 cents at one contract. The ladders showed no rule breaks at all. A vendor scanner found the same on 12 September 2026. Some college football ladders looked out of order at the midpoint, halfway between buy and sell prices. But **none could be locked in** at real prices ([predictionmarketspicks ladder scanner](https://predictionmarketspicks.com/tools/ladder-scanner)). That vendor sells subscriptions.

Links between different questions behave the same way. The largest study replayed 42 days of Polymarket order books in mid-2026 ([Shi and Zhai](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23517)). An ideal trader taking every gap after fees would have made $2.50 million on $11.1 million of capital, about 3.5% a week. But the typical gap lasted 2.9 to 31 seconds. The best price held only 30 to 40 shares. Ten accounts took 78% of the gaps anyone took. On Kalshi, nobody has studied whether 15-minute, hourly and daily crypto contracts on the same index stay consistent. Kalshi parlays are the one linked product that looks mispriced. A parlay is one bet that needs several outcomes, its legs, to all come true. Parlays cost about 3% more per extra leg than their legs bought separately ([Moshrefi](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430v1)). Only a seller collects that. On Kalshi the sellers are market makers, traders who post resting orders on both sides.

Category bias is the strongest new pricing evidence. A study of 41.6 million Kalshi trades splits markets in two ([Bartlett and O'Hara](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f243677.pdf)). Single-name markets are about one person or company: earnings-call word mentions, speech content, celebrity appearances and streaming charts. Broad-based markets cover economic data, indices, rates, and crypto and currency ranges. The paper checks calibration: whether a 70c price really wins 70% of the time. All figures below are before fees. On a $1 contract, one percentage point of win chance equals one cent.

| Market type | How much Yes is overpriced, before fees | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Single-name, all prices | 4.5 cents per contract; far too big to be luck (t ≈ 10, where about 2 is the usual bar) | Yes buyers overpay at every price level |
| Single-name, Yes priced 40c to 49c | 8.6 cents; these won only 37% of the time | The biggest gap sits where fees are highest |
| Entertainment | 3.1 cents | Same direction, smaller |
| Sports | 1.3 cents | Small |
| Weather | 1.0 cent | Small |
| Broad-based (economic data, indices, crypto ranges) | 0.2 cents | Effectively fair |

The t ≈ 10 is my division of the paper's 4.5-point estimate by its margin of error (standard error 0.44). The Wall Street Journal found the same pattern in over 35,000 Kalshi mention markets. Yes bets priced at 50% won only about 40% of the time. The average Yes bettor lost about 11% of the stake, as cited by [Bartlett and O'Hara](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f243677.pdf). Takers, traders who fill orders already on the book, show the same order by category. That comes from Jon Becker's study of 72.1 million Kalshi trades ([Becker](https://www.jbecker.dev/research/prediction-market-microstructure)). Finance takers lost about 0.08% per trade before fees. Media and world-events takers lost about 3.6%. The paper's own maker-profit table shows who wins in single-name markets, before fees. Takers who bought No netted **about +$5.3 million**. Takers who bought Yes netted **about −$9.9 million**. Those totals are my sums of the table's four cells.

Three things stand between this and a profit. First, cost. Buying No at 60c costs 1.68 cents per contract in taker fee, Kalshi's charge for trading, biggest near 50c. A taker also pays the spread, the gap between the buy and sell price. The 8.6-cent gap would leave something, but the spread in these thin markets is unmeasured. Second, adverse selection: getting picked off by someone who knows more than you. Single-name markets have the most informed trading on Kalshi. On a January 2026 earnings call, Netflix's co-CEO said "Warner Brothers", but the rules needed the exact words "Warner Bros". Yes jumped to about 90c, then rule-readers drove it below 40c ([Bartlett and O'Hara](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f243677.pdf)). Third, rule and regulator risk. Kalshi settled its $54 million "Khamenei out" market at its price from before the US strikes on Iran, not at Yes ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/fintech/khamenei-out-market-becomes-legal-headache-for-kalshi/)). Its review committee now sets prices in such cases, with no appeal ([DeFi Rate](https://defirate.com/news/kalshi-codifies-death-settlement-rule-in-cftc-filing-amid-backlash-over-iran-market/)). A September 2026 staff advisory from the CFTC, the US regulator, called mention markets "presumptively readily susceptible to manipulation" ([Government Enforcement Report](https://www.governmentenforcementreport.com/2026/10/cftc-issues-staff-advisory-on-prediction-market-contracts-tied-to-individual-conduct/)). The whole market type could shrink.

This bias may already sit inside the project's data. The project found thin 80c to 90c favourites earned +5.1 cents per contract, still unconfirmed. In single-name markets the favourite is often the No side. The Yes bias is also bigger early: 4.4 cents over the first 70% of a market's life, against 2.3 cents over its whole life ([Bartlett and O'Hara](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f243677.pdf)). And it lives in thin markets. An open-source study measured the bias as a slope. Above 1 means favourites (the likely side) are too cheap and longshots (the unlikely side) too dear. The slope was 1.18 in the least-traded third of Kalshi markets, against 1.02 in the most-traded third ([hrishi-tailor](https://github.com/hrishi-tailor/prediction-market-calibration)). Splitting the +5.1 cents by category and by side would show whether it is the same effect. **For this bot, buying No in single-name markets is the best new pricing idea, but it lives in a slower, different kind of market.**

## AI forecasters edge the market by a sliver and lose real money

The best AI models now forecast Kalshi events slightly better than Kalshi prices. The lead is tiny. On the Prophet Arena leaderboard, built on live Kalshi markets, the market scores 84.32 on a 0 to 100 accuracy scale ([Prophet Arena](https://www.prophetarena.co/leaderboard/forecast)). The top 10 model setups beat it by only 0.78 to 1.49 points. The page gives no error range for the market's score, so these gaps are not tested against luck. In the same page's betting simulation, two models that beat the market on accuracy still lost money.

The Prophet Arena team's own paper shows how fragile this is ([arXiv 2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.06166v1)). It scored five models on 200 Kalshi events. The Brier gap below measures forecast misses against the market's, so a plus sign means the model beat the market. Kelly is the bet size that grows money fastest without risking ruin, if your odds are right.

| Model | Accuracy against the market (Brier gap; plus means better) | Return, the paper's cautious bet sizing | Return, full Kelly sizing (the fastest-growth bet size) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Another frontier language model | Slightly better (+0.0016) | +22.1% | −99.9% (wiped out) |
| Gemini 3 | Slightly better (+0.0008) | +8.1% | −42.7% |
| GPT-5.2 | Worse (−0.0347) | +4.5% | −99.9% (wiped out) |
| LLaMA 4 Maverick | Worse (−0.0450) | −13.7% | −99.9% (wiped out) |
| Grok 4.1 Fast | Worse (−0.0462) | −11.6% | −99.9% (wiped out) |

Two lessons follow. Accuracy barely predicts profit: one model lost to the market on accuracy yet made money. And sizing bets by an AI's own odds is ruinous, because the models are overconfident. On 300 Kalshi questions, every model tested claimed more certainty than it earned ([KalshiBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16030)). Even the best one's stated confidence was off from how often it came true by 12 percentage points on average. That is a calibration failure: a stated 70% did not win 70% of the time.

Real-money tests are few, and mostly losing. One Kalshi test turned **$200 into $360.67 in 26 days** with Gemini 3 ([arXiv 2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.06166v1)). It filled 236 trades and paid $26.31 in fees, 13% of its starting money. One author works at Kalshi, and one month is far too short to tell skill from luck. A larger test gave six models $10,000 each on Kalshi for 57 days ([Prediction Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07355v1)). **Every model lost, from 16% to 31%.** Weather markets made up 71% to 97% of each model's settled bets. That matches the project's own failed weather tests.

News signals face a speed problem. About 3% of Polymarket accounts show lasting skill, and they trade fast on public news ([SSRN blog on Gómez-Cram et al.](https://blog.ssrn.com/2026/06/29/a-closer-look-prediction-market-accuracy-crowd-wisdom-or-informed-minority-by-roberto-gomez-cram-yunhan-guo/)). One open-source news bot found its news feeds typically saw stories 32 minutes after the first post on X ([mehek-builds](https://github.com/mehek-builds/prediction-market)). An AI call adds seconds more. A bot like that buys after the skilled traders. That is adverse selection: getting picked off by someone who knows more than you. News-reading AI can also be steered. In a 2026 test, one planted article flipped 56% of forecasts from one side of 50% to the other ([arXiv 2609.22246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22246)).

Order flow carries information, but not yet a taker profit. Order flow is the stream of buy and sell orders. On Kalshi, runs of one-sided trading predict losses for market makers, traders who post resting orders on both sides. That holds in single-name markets, about one person or company. It does not hold in broad-based markets like crypto price levels ([Stanford Law summary of Bartlett and O'Hara](https://law.stanford.edu/press/adverse-selection-in-prediction-markets-evidence-from-kalshi/)). That is a warning for makers, and mild good news for this bot's markets. On Polymarket, net buying by skilled accounts predicts final outcomes, far too strongly to be luck (t = 9.6, where about 2 is the usual bar) ([JasonJSant reproduction](https://github.com/JasonJSant/polymarket-skilled-traders)). But that signal needs account names, and Kalshi shows none. No study reports an order-flow rule that pays a taker after fees, on any prediction market. A taker is a trader who fills orders already on the book.

Blending AI with the market does a little better than the market alone. In earnings-call mention markets, a market-plus-AI blend beat the market by a small margin (Brier score 0.1392 against 0.1441; lower is better) ([arXiv 2602.21229](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.21229v2)). The gain was largest at middle prices. That is where Kalshi's taker fee, its charge for trading, peaks at 1.75 cents a contract near 50c. **For a 15-minute crypto bot, AI forecasting does not fit.** Those markets move on spot prices within seconds, not on reasoning about news.

## Conclusion

The edges (profit per contract, in cents) that survive for a small taker are mechanical, not informational. A taker fills orders already on the book. Those edges come from how a contract settles, and from who habitually buys which side. Wherever a supposedly smarter benchmark exists, Kalshi has matched or beaten it: options, fed funds futures and the Cleveland Fed nowcast. AI models edge it only by a sliver that has not become steady profit. So "trade Kalshi toward a better forecast" usually means trading against the better forecaster. The settlement average is a different kind of thing. It is not a forecast at all. It is arithmetic the rules publish, which a spot-price model (one that treats the closing price as the settle) gets wrong.

The single-name Yes bias and this bot's edge from buying favourites (the likely side) may be one effect seen twice. Casual traders overbuy the exciting outcome. Whoever sells it to them collects, whether that seller is a maker posting resting orders or a taker buying the dull side. Both real leads also live where size is small: inside a one-minute window, or in thin markets. That suits a small bankroll. It also means neither will scale far, and each will draw faster rivals or new rules once it pays.
