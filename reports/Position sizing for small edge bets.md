# Size for the edge you can prove

**3% a bet with a 20% window cap is not reckless by textbook Kelly, but it is too big for what this bot can prove.** On the bot's own realistic-fill growth curve, 3% is the peak, and in practice that makes it a full-Kelly bet. Full Kelly is the stake that maximises long-run compounding, and every serious practitioner, from Thorp to Benter, refuses to bet it. When each order fills only as far as Kalshi's trade tape allows, profit from $2,000 is almost flat between 2% and 4% a bet ($3.4k–$3.6k), while the deepest drop climbs from 33% to 57%. Half the growth-maximising size, 1.5%, keeps about 83% of the profit with a 26% deepest drop and a −12% worst day, against 45% and −21% at 3%. The bigger unknown is the edge itself. The untouched December–August history earned about 0.3 points a bet after fills and fees. The weeks since the rules were built earned about 1.2 points, and live bets at 92¢ and up about 1.8 before fees. If 0.3 points is the truth, 3%/20% is roughly full Kelly in any window with five or more correlated bets. If 1.2 points is, it is a tenth to a third of Kelly. Overbetting costs far more than underbetting, so size for the edge you can prove: **1.5% a bet, 12% a window, displayed depth as a hard ceiling, and stops that need a human to reset them at −10% in 24 hours and −20% from the peak**. Step up to 2%/15% only after about 3,000 more live bets confirm at least a one-point edge. In the backtest, every risk overlay tested (tighter window caps, correlation-scaled stakes, drawdown step-downs) did worse than simply betting a smaller flat fraction.

## Kelly asks for 12–75% a bet, which is why nobody bets it

For a contract bought at price p that pays $1 with true win probability q, the **Kelly fraction** is f* = (q − p)/(1 − p) ([Meister 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14144)). That equals 1 minus the ratio of your loss rate to the loss rate the price implies. At the bot's 1–1.5 point edge, full Kelly is **12.5–19% of bankroll at 92¢, 20–30% at 95¢ and 50–75% at 98¢**. MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba list this as a "bad property" of Kelly: "the bets may be a large fraction of current wealth when the wager is favorable and the risk of loss is very small". Their example is a 64%-Kelly favourite that Thorp and Ziemba actually bet "with a low fractional Kelly wager" ([MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba 2010](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf)). The same formula shows why. The stake moves 1/(1 − p) points of bankroll for every point of error in the win rate, so a one-point mistake shifts the Kelly stake by 12.5 points of bankroll at 92¢ and by 50 at 98¢. Fees count as a higher price. Kalshi's taker fee of $0.07 × P × (1 − P) per contract ([Bürgi, Deng & Whelan](https://www.karlwhelan.com/Papers/Kalshi.pdf)) is about 0.5¢ at 92¢ and 0.27¢ at 96¢, which is a quarter to a half of a one-point edge.

**Fractional Kelly**, a fixed share of the Kelly stake, is the professional answer. Thorp shows that half Kelly keeps three-quarters of the growth rate and cuts the chance of ever losing half the bankroll from 1/2 to 1/8. He reports that "most people strongly prefer" about half Kelly ([Thorp 2006](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-thorp.pdf)). Benter, whose Hong Kong racing syndicate is the best-documented Kelly operation, warns that overestimating the edge by a factor of two makes Kelly betting lose money, and that this "is easily done in practice". He recommends 1/2 or 1/3 Kelly ([Benter 1994](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1994-benter.pdf)). Baker and McHale show formally that the bet "should be shrunk" when the win probability is itself an estimate ([Baker & McHale 2013](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/deca.2013.0271)). Errors in the mean (the edge) matter roughly ten times more than errors in variance ([MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba 2010](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf)). Strongly skewed contracts like these leave even less room for error. Computed exactly from the log-growth formula for 92–98¢ contracts with a 1–1.5 point edge, half Kelly keeps only 64–74% of maximum growth, and growth turns negative at 1.3–1.9× Kelly, not 2×. Busseti, Ryu and Boyd's drawdown-constrained Kelly turns a stated risk limit into a bet size. "At most a 10% chance of ever losing half" works out to about half Kelly on these contracts ([Busseti, Ryu & Boyd 2016](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/papers/pdf/kelly.pdf)).

The hard part is the edge, not the formula. There are three estimates, and they disagree by a factor of six. The tape-fill backtest earned **+3.4¢ per $10 staked over Dec 10–Jul 17 and +3.1¢ over Jul 18–Aug 23**, both periods untouched by rule-building. That is about 0.3 points at the bot's typical 96¢ price, and neither 95% interval excludes zero. It earned +6.2¢ over Aug 24–Sep 23, when the rules were built, and **+12.3¢ (95% CI +5.1 to +19.6) since Sep 24**, about 1.2 points ([per-period rerun](<../research_notes/Position sizing for small edge bets/scripts/grid_reruns_output.txt>)). Live bets at 92¢ and up lost 18 times against 33.5 implied by price, about 1.8 points before fees (roughly 1.5 after) over 876 bets ([local grid](<../research_notes/Position sizing for small edge bets/local_backtest_sizing_grid.md>)). Those 876 bets are worth fewer independent observations than they look. Bets in a window come in clusters: 2.6 per active window on average in the backtest, and if one favourite loses, another in the same window loses 36% of the time. That makes the **design effect** (how much clustering inflates the variance) about 2. The live record is therefore worth roughly 430 independent bets, and the shortfall in losses is about 1.9 standard errors: real, but not settled. Pinning a one-point edge to about 2 standard errors takes about 3,000 more bets, or roughly two weeks at the current ~220–235 filled bets a day at 92¢ and up (Oct 12–13; corrected Oct 14 from an earlier "eight weeks at ~55 a day"). Benter's warning cuts one way: "many factors may cause one's real-time advantage to be less than past simulations would suggest, and very few can cause it to be greater." Short-dated crypto edges also decay fast. Polymarket put taker fees on its 15-minute crypto markets in January 2026, reportedly to blunt latency bots ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/384461/polymarket-adds-taker-fees-to-15-minute-crypto-markets-to-fund-liquidity-rebates)).

The trader's own sizing grid is the most decision-relevant evidence here. The "tape" model fills each order only as far as Kalshi's public trade tape says the book had; it predicted 126 contracts against 130 real on 76 live orders ([local grid](<../research_notes/Position sizing for small edge bets/local_backtest_sizing_grid.md>)). The pattern is the textbook Kelly curve. **Profit peaks near 3% and falls on either side, while the deepest drop rises steadily with size.** A growth peak is, by definition, full Kelly, so 3% is the bot's realised full-Kelly stake under realistic fills. Half of it, 1.5%, keeps about 83% of the profit and cuts the deepest drop from 45% to 26%, close to Thorp's three-quarters-of-growth, far-less-drawdown trade. The untouched seven months are the stress test. **Only 1% made money there. 1.5% broke even, and 3% lost 13%.** That is the overbetting signature: if twice Kelly gives zero growth, Kelly in that regime was under 1%. The recent months reward bigger sizes (+48% at 3% against +28% at 1.5%). That is the real price of caution if the recent edge holds.

Adverse selection explains why profit stops scaling with size. **Adverse selection** means fills come disproportionately when the bet is worse. Across the whole tape run, the dollar-weighted return falls from **3.5¢ per $10 staked at 1% to 2.4¢ at 3%**, and the fill share falls from 46% to 36%. Bigger orders pick up extra contracts mainly when the book is deep, and the book is deep when nobody faster wanted those contracts. The live record shows the same thing directly. The 292 live orders that got nothing **won 97.6% of the time, 7 losses against 14.9 implied**, so the winners are the ones that run away before the bot's order lands ([Live capture review](<Live capture review Oct 13.md>)). This is standard microstructure. In Glosten–Milgrom the quote already prices informed flow ([Glosten & Milgrom 1985](https://www.edegan.com/pdfs/Glosten%20Milgrom%20%281985%29%20-%20Bid%20Ask%20and%20Transaction%20Prices%20in%20a%20Specialist%20Market%20with%20Heterogeneously%20Informed%20Trades.pdf)). Finnish retail investors' poor returns trace largely to adversely selected limit-order fills ([Linnainmaa 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v65y2010i4p1473-1506.html)). A 2026 crypto execution study found signals that *would not* have filled passively returned +4.9 bps, against −4.3 bps for those that would have ([The Microstructure Lab](https://themicrostructurelab.substack.com/p/execution-simulation-for-hf-directional)). For sizing, Kelly's first-order condition counts only the states in which the marginal contract actually fills. So the stake must come from the **fill-weighted win rate**, not the signal's overall win rate, and it ends up smaller.

Depth, not bankroll, is the other ceiling. Benter found that "a successful betting operation will soon find that all of its bets are pool-size-limited". In his example, betting two-thirds of the profit-maximising amount still earned 90% of the maximum profit ([Benter 1994](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1994-benter.pdf)). QuantConnect's backtester caps crypto capacity at the average size quoted at the best bid and ask ([QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/lean-engine/statistics/capacity)), and the crypto execution study's rule is "size orders against the book, not the account" ([The Microstructure Lab](https://themicrostructurelab.substack.com/p/execution-simulation-for-hf-directional)). Live fill share was flat at **57–64% for $10–40 orders** ([Live capture review](<Live capture review Oct 13.md>)). At $2,000, 1.5% is a $30 order, inside that tested range. 3% is $60, outside it. When depth binds, the extra bankroll should sit idle. Raising the percentage only adds contracts from the deep, worse states.

One consequence deserves emphasis. The 60% fill share is currently an **accidental de-risker**. Fast entry went live on Oct 14, and the +1¢ limit filled 95.6% of contracts on paper against 62% real ([Live capture review](<Live capture review Oct 13.md>)), so fills are heading toward the full-fill line. On that line, 3%/20% has a **56% deepest drop and a −35.7% worst 24 hours**, enough to trip the bot's own 35% halt. 1.5% has 32% and −20% ([local grid](<../research_notes/Position sizing for small edge bets/local_backtest_sizing_grid.md>); [per-period rerun](<../research_notes/Position sizing for small edge bets/scripts/grid_reruns_output.txt>)). Better execution means each percentage point carries more risk, so the stake should fall as fills improve.

## Ten correlated favourites carry the risk of two or three bets

Thorp's two-bet example sets the rule: at correlation +1, each of two bets gets half the single-bet Kelly, so the pair together is one Kelly bet. He notes that blackjack hands at the same table, correlated around 0.5, "should substantially reduce the Kelly fraction per hand" ([Thorp 2006](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-thorp.pdf)). Medo, Yeung and Zhang generalise this. With uniform pairwise correlation ρ, n bets carry the Kelly weight of n/(1 + (n − 1)ρ) independent bets, which can never exceed 1/ρ ([Medo et al. 2009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0805.3397)). The bot's 36% co-loss rate converts to a binary correlation of about 0.33. So **ten crypto favourites in one window are worth about 2.5 independent bets**, and no number of extra coins pushes that past 3. Medo et al. also find the errors asymmetric: underestimating correlation "decreases investment performance dramatically", while overestimating it costs little. Thorp adds that pairwise correlation is not enough for an exact answer; the full joint distribution of outcomes is needed. Busseti, Ryu and Boyd show the joint problem is a convex optimisation that, at equal drawdown risk, beats uniform fractional Kelly on growth: 0.047 against 0.035 in their example ([Busseti, Ryu & Boyd 2016](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/papers/pdf/kelly.pdf)).

Institutions handle correlated groups the same way: treat the group as one position. The Basel large-exposure rule caps a bank's exposure to "a group of connected counterparties" (ones "likely to fail simultaneously") at 25% of Tier 1 capital ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsisummaries/lex.htm)). UCITS funds pair a 5–10% per-issuer limit with a 40% cap on the sum of the larger holdings ([CISI guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cisi-icwim/collective-investment-schemes/ucits-and-aifmd-regimes)), and Thorp lists per-position and total limits as standard additions to Kelly. The 3%/20% rule has the right structure but the wrong scaling. Its Kelly multiple roughly triples as a window fills (0.40× to 1.11× in the pessimistic row of the first table), because the cap binds only at seven or more bets, about 6% of active windows. Two refinements follow from theory. First, independent clusters add up, so metals and energy contracts should get their own budget rather than share the crypto window's ([Medo et al. 2009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0805.3397)). Second, under a binding cap, the growth-optimal split fills the highest-edge bets first. In the research model that beat an equal split by about 27% in growth, but only if the edge ranking is reliable.

The backtest pushes back on going further. At 2% a bet, tightening the window cap from 20% to 10% cost 14% of the profit and left the deepest drop unchanged at 33%. Scaling stakes by correlation, 2% ÷ (1 + 0.33 × (n − 1)), ended at **$2,865 with a 24% deepest drop, worse than a flat 1% ($3,065, 18%)** ([cap and correlation reruns](<../research_notes/Position sizing for small edge bets/scripts/grid_reruns_output.txt>)). In this history, crowded windows were not worse bets than lone ones, and the worst 15 minutes at 2% cost 8.2%. The window cap's job is therefore the window not yet seen, such as one sharp crypto move that flips every favourite at once. With independent losses, five of ten 95¢ favourites failing together is a 0.006% event. At the measured correlation, the research model puts it at about 3% of full windows. The cap should rarely bind but should bound that case. At 1.5% a bet, **a 12% cap binds in about 2% of active windows** (nine or more bets) and cost about 4% of the profit against the current 20%.

## Professionals cap single bets at 1–3% and make stops human-reset

Professional bettors sit well below the bot's current per-bet size. Billy Walters uses a 1% unit, scales from half a unit for his smallest edges to a hard **maximum of 3 units (3%) per event** for the biggest ([Covers](https://www.covers.com/guides/betting-tips-from-pro-sports-bettor-billy-walters); [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/billy-walters-sports-betting/)). The bot spends Walters' ceiling on every bet, up to six at once before the window cap starts trimming. Unabated calls 1% flat staking "the norm" when the edge is uncertain and recommends a half or a quarter of Kelly otherwise ([Unabated](https://unabated.com/articles/how-to-build-a-sports-betting-bankroll)). Buchdahl's simulations show half Kelly cutting the chance of halving a bankroll from about 10% to about 1%. Believing a 5% edge that is really 2.5% turns the bettor into "a coin-tossing machine" ([Buchdahl](https://www.football-data.co.uk/blog/kelly_staking.php)). Systematic funds apply the same haircut. Carver sizes to at most half Kelly and multiplies back-tested Sharpe ratios by about 0.75 before trusting them ([CXO Advisory on Carver](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)).

Prediction-market evidence says favourites are a thin edge, not a free one. On Kalshi, makers averaged −9.64% against −31.46% for takers. Contracts above 70¢ show "statistically significant, though small, positive post-fee returns", which makes them the one corner where a taker like this bot can win ([Bürgi, Deng & Whelan](https://www.karlwhelan.com/Papers/Kalshi.pdf)). On Polymarket, winners mostly provide liquidity, while takers lose ([Akey et al., via HEC](https://www.hec.edu/en/faculty-research/events/who-wins-and-who-loses-prediction-markets-evidence-polymarket)). Accounts with more than 50 orders a day finished $246.8M ahead while 69% of retail accounts finished behind ([Galaxy via Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/crypto/articles/69-retail-polymarket-traders-finish-045227939.html)). The documented blow-ups are all concentration failures. One trader reportedly lost $6.5M in a day when a "won't happen" geopolitical bet happened; the source notes the figure is unverified ([CryptoAdventure](https://cryptoadventure.com/polymarket-whales-6-5m-wipeout-sparks-biggest-loss-talk/)). Another lost $2M in 35 days, mostly on one match, prompting a suggested 3–5% per-event cap ([BeInCrypto](https://beincrypto.com/polymarket-trader-loss-risk-management/)). Kalshi market makers lost about $22.4M pre-fee on one NBA game ([InGame](https://ingame.com/susquehanna-biggest-sports-kalshi-volume-knicks)). Guides to buying 93–99¢ "bonds" give no fixed percentage. Their rule is to size "assuming a loss will eventually occur", since at 95¢ one loss erases about 19 wins ([startpolymarket](https://startpolymarket.com/strategies/bonding/)).

Firm risk limits are mostly about structure, not numbers. The FIA's guidance for automated traders calls for pre-trade limits at several levels of aggregation and a repeated-execution throttle that stays disabled "until an authorized person re-enables it". It also calls for a **kill switch** that cancels resting orders and blocks new ones, blocking any order that has no size limit set, and blocking orders when market data looks stale ([FIA 2024](https://fia.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/FIA_WP_AUTOMATED%20TRADING%20RISK%20CONTROLS_FINAL_0.pdf)). Knight Capital shows why. It sent more than 4 million orders and lost over $460M in 45 minutes after 97 warning emails went unread ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222)). An alert that only notifies is not a control. The bot's 35%-in-24-hours halt is about seven times FTMO's 5% daily limit, which makes it a catastrophe stop, not a risk budget. If it clears itself when the 24-hour window rolls off, it is a pause, not a kill switch. The bot's PC clock drift is the bot's version of FIA's stale-data problem: orders were landing a median 4.1 s after minute 8, and each extra second cost about 2.6 points of fill share ([Live capture review](<Live capture review Oct 13.md>)). A clock-offset check that blocks orders is the matching control.

**Drawdown-based sizing**, shrinking stakes as the account falls from its peak, has rigorous roots. Grossman and Zhou show that bets proportional to the cushion above a floor that trails the peak are optimal when drawdowns are capped ([Grossman & Zhou 1993](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v3y1993i3p241-276.html)). Kaminski and Lo, however, prove that stop-loss rules always lower expected return when returns are independent over time. They help only when losses come in persistent regimes ([Kaminski & Lo 2014](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114876)). The bot's history has regimes, yet the step-downs still lost. At 2% a bet, halving stakes at −15% from the peak ended at **$2,774 with a 28% deepest drop**, and a Grossman–Zhou cushion with a 35% floor ended at $3,172 with 26%. **A flat 1.5% beat both: $3,330 with 26%** ([drawdown rerun](<../research_notes/Position sizing for small edge bets/scripts/grid_reruns_output.txt>)). Drawdown triggers should therefore detect model breaks and call a human, not resize bets automatically. At 2%, the backtest fell 10% or more within 24 hours three times in ten months; at 3%, twelve times. That is a sensible review frequency.

## Conclusion

For this bot, the sizing question is mostly about edge estimation and execution, not risk appetite. Kelly theory, practitioner rules and firm limits all point the same way: size to the edge you can defend and let depth set the ceiling. The bot's own data adds two things the literature could not. First, under realistic fills, 3% is already the growth peak, so the only thing larger stakes buy is variance. Second, the fill shortfall has been doing quiet risk management. As fast entry and the +1¢ limit push fills toward 100%, the same 3% carries the full-fill line's 56% deepest drop, so better execution should come with smaller stakes, not bigger ones.

The more surprising lesson is that simplicity won. Tighter window caps, correlation-scaled stakes and drawdown step-downs each looked sound in theory, and each did worse in the backtest than a smaller flat fraction. The one structural limit worth keeping is a window cap set as a tail backstop. That will likely hold as long as edge uncertainty, not correlation, is the dominant risk; correlation-aware sizing becomes worth its complexity only once roughly 3,000 more live bets have pinned the edge down. Until then, extra capital beyond what the book can absorb is better spent on new, independent markets than on bigger orders into the same thin 15-minute books.
